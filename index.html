Revealing Patterns in Experimental Data

Physicists hate curves
Here's why, and what they do about it.

Watch the ball above. It speeds up as it rolls down the ramp; it is accelerating. When you measure its distance and time and plot the results, you get a curve.

distance s (m) vs time t (s)

A curve looks interesting. But for a physicist, a curve is actually a problem, and in this lesson you'll find out exactly why.

You'll learn a powerful technique called linearisation, which physicists use to turn a curve into a straight line. By extracting the slope, using the formula you already know from coordinate geometry, you will use its value to uncover a hidden physical reality about the world.

Screen 4 of 11: the problem

Why curves are frustrating

The slope of a curve is different at every single point. The three coloured dashed lines show the slope at three different positions on the curve; they are all different values.

In physics, the slope of a graph always represents something physical: a speed, a resistance, a refractive index. If the slope is different everywhere, we cannot identify that one physical quantity.

We need a straight line. And to get a straight line, we need to think carefully about what the physics actually tells us.
Screen 5 of 11: the equation

What the physics tells us

For a ball accelerating uniformly from rest, the relationship between distance and time is:

s = ½at²

This means s is directly proportional to t², not to t itself. So plotting s against t gives a curve. But plotting s against t² should give a straight line through the origin.

Screen 7 of 11: the slope

Calculating the slope

Where does s = ½at² come from?

If you open your official Log Tables (Formulas and Tables booklet) to the Mechanics section, you will find the kinematic equation for displacement:

s = ut + ½at²

In this experiment, the ball is released from a stationary position. Because it starts from rest, its initial velocity is exactly zero, so u = 0.

Substituting u = 0 into the formula:

s = (0 × t) + ½at²

The first term disappears completely, simplifying the model to:

s = ½at²

This is the exact reason why we plot s on the vertical axis against t² on the horizontal axis.

Here is the straight line graph with two points marked with stars. We use the slope formula from coordinate geometry:

The general formula you already know:

slope = y₂ − y₁x₂ − x₁

For this graph, y is distance s and x is time squared t². So the formula becomes:

slope = s₂ − s₁t₂² − t₁²
Point 1  (x₁, y₁):  t₁² = 1 s²,  s₁ = 0.5 m
Point 2  (x₂, y₂):  t₂² = 25 s²,  s₂ = 12.5 m

Screen 8 of 11: the meaning

What does the slope represent physically?

The slope of the graph is ½. Now let's see how the physics equation maps onto the straight line formula y = mx + c.

Both equations side by side:

s = 12at²
y = mx + c

Our line passes through the origin, so c = 0. We can write + 0 to make the parallel clearer:

s = 12at² + 0
y = mx + c

The blue circle connects + 0 to + c. The line passes through the origin so c = 0:

s = 12at² + 0 
y = mx + c 
c = 0  →  straight line through the origin ✓

The green circle connects ½a to m. The slope of our graph equals ½a:

s =  12a t² + 0 
y =  m x + c 
slope = ½a  →  the slope tells us the acceleration!
c = 0  →  line through the origin

The mapping shows that slope = ½a. Rearranging to find a:

slope = ½a
2 × slope = a
a = 2 × slope
a = 2 × ½
a = ms⁻² ✓

The general pattern

Linearisation works the same way for every equation in physics. Find what y is proportional to, put that on the x-axis, and you get a straight line. Then use y₂−y₁x₂−x₁ to find the slope, and interpret it physically.

Notice that in every case the slope formula is exactly the same: y₂−y₁x₂−x₁. What changes is only what y and x represent physically.

Your question every time you get a graph:

Is this a straight line?
If yes, note that y is directly proportional to x, use y₂−y₁x₂−x₁ to find the slope, then ask what it represents physically.
If no, what transformation will give me a straight line?

The physics changes. The method never does.
Today, the slope gives you a way to calculate acceleration.
Next time, a different graph's slope will give you the refractive index of glass, the threshold frequency of a metal, or the stiffness of a spring
Screen 11 of 11: real-world data

From ideal to real

The data in this lesson was idealised, meaning it was perfectly clean and free from error, so that the mathematical pattern was clear. In the school laboratory, using real equipment, your data will be messy.

Factors such as friction on the ramp, air resistance, and small variations in electronic timers or ticker tape mean your t² values will not lie exactly on a straight line.

Real experimental data: points scatter around the line

Your points may not line up perfectly.

The line might not pass exactly through the origin (0, 0).

How do physicists handle this?

We do not force the line through points or connect the dots. Instead, we draw a line of best fit. This line averages out the random errors of the experiment, giving an accurate slope and allowing us to extract the true value of the physical quantity despite the limitations of real equipment.

Rules for a line of best fit
  • Use a ruler and draw one straight line through the general trend of the points.
  • Aim to have roughly equal numbers of points above and below the line.
  • The line does not need to pass through any specific data point.
  • Do not connect the dots.
  • Calculate the slope using two points on the line, not two data points from the table.
